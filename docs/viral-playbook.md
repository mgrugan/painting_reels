# What the viral reels actually do

Measured from five reels that performed, frame by frame. This is the reference
the script prompt is written against; when the prompt and this file disagree,
this file is the evidence and the prompt is wrong.

## Length — longer than expected

| Reel | Length | Shots | Avg shot |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Problem We All Live With | 1:58 | 15 | 7.8s |
| The Shadow | 1:14 | 12 | 6.2s |
| The Starry Night | 1:45 | 11 | 9.6s |
| Death playing chess | 1:27 | 10 | 8.7s |
| Mona Lisa | 1:40 | 10 | 10.0s |

Average 1:37. **None is short.** "Too long and boring" is not a length problem,
it is a density problem: a boring reel is one where a beat says something the
viewer could already see.

## The shot-length curve — front-loaded fast, back-loaded slow

Gaps between cuts, in order, for the two clearest cases:

```
The Problem We All Live With:  4.1  4.0  7.4  5.1 │ 11.2  7.9 10.7 13.2 12.5  9.5  8.5 10.8  6.9  3.1
The Shadow:                    5.2  4.9  4.4  5.4 │  8.5  5.1  7.7  6.8  6.9 10.5  4.5
```

Four of five open with a shot of **4–5.3 seconds**. The first third is staccato —
short lines, quick cuts, almost a list. Then shots stretch to **9–14 seconds** as
the captions get longer and start carrying meaning rather than description.

The old build did the opposite: a 7.5s hook and uniform 7.5s beats throughout.
That is slow at the start, where attention is won or lost, and rushed at the end,
where the thinking happens.

## The shape of a reel

1. **Hook**, over the whole painting, usually on black. They reuse one template
   almost every time:
   > "Now I understand why people spend hours staring at the same painting in museums."

   Four of the five open with that exact line. It says nothing about the painting.
   It is about *the act of looking*, and it flatters the viewer for doing it. The
   one variant is the same move in a different key:
   > "Millions of people stare at her smile, but Leonardo da Vinci wanted you to notice something else first."

   — you are looking at the wrong thing, and I know what the right thing is.

2. **Three or four fast, flat beats** naming what is in the picture. Very short:
   > "She is a Black girl." / "And walks to school carrying their notebook" / "Her name is Ruby Bridges."

   Or a **subversion**, which is the strongest opener they use:
   > "this looks romantic. But this is Not.."

3. **The long middle.** Facts and dramatic readings, mixed, getting longer. The
   recurring sentence shape is *object → meaning*, almost always with an
   instruction to look:
   > "Look at the small hourglass on the table, bc every grain of sand is another move the young man can never get back."
   > "Look closely at her eyes, because they seem to follow you no matter where you stand."

4. **An interpretive payoff.** Never a fact. The emotional claim the whole reel
   was built to earn:
   > "Because shadows stay… after people disappear."
   > "She is already grieving a man who is still alive."

5. **A comment prompt**, every time:
   > "Let me know in the comments if I missed any details."
   > "I'd love to know what you noticed first.. let me know in the comments!"

6. **A follow card.**

**There is no title card.** The painting is never named on screen — not the title,
not the date, not the museum. The artist gets named mid-reel only when it makes
the story better ("Leonardo created this effect…", "Van Gogh painted a Dutch
church from memories of home"). Withholding the title as a reveal, which the old
build treated as the point of the format, is not what these do at all.

## Voice

- First person, present tense, casual. "Now I understand…", "I'd love to know…"
- Second person constantly. "Look closely", "no matter where you stand", "you".
- Written like a person typing, not an institution: "bc" for because, trailing
  "..", sentences starting lowercase, the odd loose grammar. It reads as someone
  telling you something, not a caption written by a museum.
- Emotional vocabulary, unhedged: heartbreaking, grieving, never return,
  disappear, can never get back.
- Short lines. Three or four words per line on screen, 2–5 lines per beat.

## The one line that must not be crossed

The drama is in the *framing*, never in the *facts*. "Shadows stay after people
disappear" is an interpretation of a real gesture in a real painting. "Van Gogh
painted it in a mental hospital from the view outside his window" is true. The
sensational register works because the underlying claims hold up — an account
built on invented facts about famous paintings gets corrected in the comments,
and the comments are the distribution.

So: dramatise the language, the pacing and the reading. Never the record.
